# Writing rules

Self-contained. Do not invoke another writing skill.

## Rank before writing

Section order never changes. The order of items inside a section always does.

Before writing a section, judge what the reader would act on first and lead with it, then the rest in descending order.

This matters most in `Risks and blockers`. Four risks as four identically shaped bullets says they are equally important, which is almost never true and is usually the writer declining to decide. In one real CFX report the four were: a capacity conflict across two orgs that would slip a market into the next quarter, two defects the squad could fix itself, and a stalled rollout. Leading with the first is the judgement the report exists to carry.

It also helps the reader upstream, who wants one risk sentence and will take the first one.

`Metrics` leads with the metric that moved. `Up next` leads with the nearest commitment. A section whose items could be shuffled with no loss is a genuine list, and there the order carries nothing.

## Every sentence earns its place

After drafting, take each clause and ask whether removing it changes what the reader would do or believe. If not, delete it. Not shorten it.

Patterns that survive ordinary editing because each feels like it adds something:

- **Restating the heading.** Under "Risks", opening with "There are several risks."
- **Scene-setting.** "As the team continues to scale the rollout across markets…" Start at the fact.
- **The appositive that adds nothing.** "Talabat finished on Sep 15, which puts it alongside Glovo and HungerStation at effectively complete." The table already says that.
- **Adjectives of degree.** Significant, substantial, considerable, key, critical. If the number is there, the adjective is noise. If it is not, find it.
- **Trailing summary.** A last sentence restating the paragraph. Paragraphs end when the point lands.
- **Process narration.** "We looked into this and found that X." Say X.

When a section runs long, **cut content rather than compressing language**. Compressing produces dense unreadable text and keeps the material that should have gone.

## Form

| Use | When |
|---|---|
| Prose | Items differ in importance or one causes another. `Status`, `Risks and blockers`, `Decisions needed`. |
| Bullets | Items are discrete and parallel. `Up next`. |
| Table | Each item has two or more attributes. `Metrics`, `Rollout`. |

Bullets front-load too: the point first, context after. A bullet longer than two lines is a paragraph wearing a bullet.

## Plain English

- The shorter word. "Use" not "utilize". "Show" not "demonstrate".
- Active voice. "The team missed the target", not "the target was missed".
- The point first, in every paragraph.
- One idea per paragraph.
- Name things. "Contact rate dropped 4 points", not "performance declined". "Glovo and Talabat", not "several entities".
- No hedging. Say what the sources show.

## Never

**Punctuation:** em-dashes, semicolons. Rewrite the sentence.

**Constructions:** "not only X but also Y", "not just X but Y", "X is not about Y, it's about Z", and the theatrical short sentence after a long one.

**Filler:** "it's worth noting that", "it's important to mention", "in conclusion", and "furthermore" or "moreover" as transitions.

**Buzzwords:** leverage, utilize, facilitate, robust, seamless, comprehensive, impactful, foundational, holistic, synergy, ecosystem, operational lift.

**Gap narration:** "owner not stated", "no blockers this week", "data unavailable". Omit instead.

## Privacy

Never in a report: customer names, order IDs, addresses, phone numbers, rider names, or individual employee names in a performance context. Aggregate numbers only.

Naming who owns a blocker is ordinary business and is fine. Naming who caused one is not.

The gatherers strip this at the source. This is the second check, not the first.
